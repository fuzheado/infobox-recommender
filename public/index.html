About the infobox recommender

This tool helps answer a question every Wikipedia editor eventually faces: “this article has no infobox — should it have one, and if so which template?” Rather than guessing from the article itself, it runs a census over the article’s peers — the articles most like it — and reports what infobox practice actually looks like there.

How it works

  1. Discover peers from three signals: Wikidata P31 (instance-of) siblings (articles of the same class, across languages), the article’s most specific categories (smallest by member count — the sharpest genre pointers), and WikiProject banners on the talk page (which project standardizes this subject’s infobox).
  2. Census: for every peer, fetch its template list and count infobox-family templates — what share of peers have any infobox, and which templates dominate.
  3. Decide: turn the census into one of three verdicts, each shipped with its evidence.

Two questions, two answers

The tool answers two separate questions, because they have separate evidence: should this article have an infobox at all? (its peers' coverage) and if so, which template? (whether one template dominates). A split vote on the second question no longer hides a clear answer to the first.

Recommend an infobox No infobox customary Weak / mixed
  • Recommend an infobox — most peers have one (70% or more of the peer pool). The template line then names the dominant box when one dominates (≥ half of the boxed peers); when none does, it names a best candidate and says so (e.g. best candidate {{Infobox artwork}}, 74% of the closest peers).
  • No infobox customary — peers are overwhelmingly bare; adding one would make the article the outlier (trace fossils: 1% of peers boxed).
  • Weak / mixed — 15–70% of peers carry an infobox, which is not decisive genre-wide. The report still names the dominant template, so "if you do add one, this is what peers use" is available; the tool just does not present it as a recommendation. A WikiProject banner may point to the standardized answer.

Reading the report

Every verdict carries its evidence: the share of peers with an infobox (coverage), the template distribution with bars, the same-class vs category-only split, and examples of boxed and bare peers — so you can weigh the argument yourself. Every template name links to its documentation, and every report has a shareable URL you can paste into a talk-page discussion.

Why is this advisory?

“The use of infoboxes is neither required nor prohibited for any article. Whether to include an infobox… is determined through discussion and consensus among the editors at each individual article.”
— Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Infoboxes (MOS:INFOBOXUSE)

MOS explicitly weighs arguments based on what similar articles do. This tool produces exactly that evidence — it never edits articles and never decides. Per the Manual of Style's own 2026 census (a Quarry-based count of template transclusions), approximately 77% of enwiki articles carry an infobox; the remaining ~23% include many that are appropriately without one.

How well does it work?

The pipeline is evaluated against an 88-case corpus: 64 labeled cases from the infobox-request backlog (labels = templates editors actually chose) plus 24 manual/canonical validate cases (sets with universal boxes — presidents, states, foods, TV episodes, …): 57 correct / 6 near-miss or defensible disagreements / 25 honest abstentions — 90% accuracy on decisive verdicts.
